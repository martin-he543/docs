In the thought experiment put forward by Plato as an attack on empiricism, he imagines three prisoners from birth are imprisoned in a cave, so that they can only gaze upon the shadows in front of them. Unknown to these prisoners, behind them is a fire and a raised walkway where people walk freely (for some reason feeling unsympathetic to the prisoners) cast their shadows ‘like showmen with their puppets.’ They believe that the sounds derive from the shadows. This is reality for these prisoners (similar to the Hindu notion of Maya): they have never seen anything else and do not realise that the shadows are merely inspired from the living things. One of these prisoners one day, however, manages to free himself. What he sees fascinates him and he is dazzled by the light from the fire and how clear the objects now are. He realises the resemblance of the shadows and the real objects. He travels up the long path through the tunnel to an even brighter light. Once up there he sees a host of plants and animals; many questions arise. He realises he was blind to the truth, but now he can see. He goes back down to the cave to inform his friends of this truth. They, completely unaware of anything other than what they have seen kill him due to his heretical views. This is an idea echoed through films and ideas. As said in the Matrix, “some are so helplessly dependant on the system that they would fight to protect it.’’ Descartes further undermines the sense by saying, “How can you trust your senses if they are so inaccurate (bent stick in water)’’ or Aristotle’s ‘tabula rasa’ tells of how we learn facts solely from the senses, so our perception of the world is complete based upon empiricism (rather than rationalism).
