A materialist or an agnostic would agree with the statement “theism is unreasonable and incoherent.” They would cite flaws in the understandings of the two largest world religions of their respective deities – Christianity and Islam. They would firstly critique the commonly-held theistic belief in their deities possessing one of the following attributes: omnipotence (all-powerful), omnibenevolence (all-loving) or omniscience (all-knowing).  For instance, regarding an omnipotent God, a conventional thought experiment (“Paradox of the Stone”) contemplates whether an omnipotent deity could conjure a stone so heavy that the deity could fail to lift. Either way, the deity fails to maintain its status of omnipotence as failing to conjure a stone results in means it isn’t omnipotent, while it succeeding in doing so similar results in the deity not being omnipotent. A similar paradox concerns omniscience – if a God is all-knowing, then this God is aware of all future actions and events, and as such, brings to light a sobering thought – are we even capable of making our own decisions? And would an omnibenevolent God allow for this to happen? Secondly, they would criticise Islamic theology. While the Quran states that anthropomorphising Allah is committing Shirk (“the sin of association”) - the greatest sin in Islam – on the other hand, throughout the Quran, Allah is referred in many different anthropomorphised comparisons, such as Allah being referred to as the “creator and sustainer of the world” (Rabb). As such, it appears, the Quran is committing Shirk, an apparently blatant incoherency in Islam. They might also criticise the apparent tri-theistic nature of the Christian deity. The Holy Trinity seems to suggest that within the God, are three separate parts, that seems to contradict the view of a monotheistic divinely simple God. Therefore, the following seem to highlight apparent inconsistencies in theism. Theism could also be said to be unreasonable (unable to be reasoned). By describing a deity using “via positiva” language (cataphatic theology), describing what a deity is, we encounter many logical paradoxes, and so hence, the only to truly understand a God is through “via negativa” language, describing what a deity is not, as condoned by Dionysius. However, by using via negative language, it becomes very difficult to understand what a deity is, and theology becomes a matter of “blind faith”, believing in something without any reason or logical support. Hence, religion could be said to be unreasonable.

On the other hand, theists such as Muslims or Christians would disagree with the above statement. They would argue that the Divine Paradoxes demonstrate a lacking in human language, rather than issues with the divine attributes. For instance, concerning omnipotence, they would argue that rather than the action of creating a stone too heavy to lift being an action that God cannot do, it is an action that cannot be done. Or perhaps even, God is beyond logic or human understanding, and so cannot be understood in human terms, or else, a conflation fallacy may arise. A Muslim would respond to the statement concerning the Quran, stating, while Allah is beyond human understanding, these terms are God-given exceptions (on the Night of Power, Mohammed wrote word for word what Allah commanded), and so may be employed without committing Shirk. They might also point out, that they are employing analogous language, so Muslims can talk about Allah in a modified Via Positiva way. A Christian would respond to a materialist, agnostic, or even a Muslim by saying that inconsistencies in the understanding of the Holy Trinity arise from straw man fallacies. They are outlined by the Trinitarian Heresies, for instance Modalism, viewing the Trinity like the Hindu Trimurti. The widely-accepted Christian view on the Trinity is “one in essence but three in person” (imago dei). They are, rather than different parts, different functions (as suggested by St Augustine) – like in the mind there are different functions such as memory, will and speech - and so there are no logical contradictions. In response to the unreasonability of theism, they would suggest that “faith is not contrary to reason.” Rather, “faith seeks understanding”. So by employing the analogous language, we can gain a reasonable understanding of God, while admitting it is an anthropomorphised one. This way, we do not succumb to the agnosticism of the Via Negativa, nor the paradoxes of the Via Positiva.

An evaluation of both views suggests that the latter of the two arguments are the more cogent. This is because firstly, it effectively manages to counter the criticisms put forward in the first paragraph by citing various fallacies, such as the straw man and conflation fallacies. Secondly, it shows how while on prima facie, it may appear that theism is incoherent and unreasonable, in reality, there are plausible explanations to why someone may misperceive certain aspects of a religion, especially regarding the use of language.

In conclusion, given the latter evaluation, it should be evident that the statement is unreasonable and this is largely because of the series fallacies which it commits, such as a conflation fallacy in regard to omnipotence, and a straw man fallacy in regard to the Holy Trinity. As such, Theism is reasonable and coherent.
