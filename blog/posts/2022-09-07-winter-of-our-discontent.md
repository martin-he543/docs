## Introduction
The Winter of 1978 into 1979 was often considered at the time for Britain, its *"Winter of Discontent",* a year prior to Margaret Thatcher's usurption to the role as Britain's first female, and longest-serving, Prime Minister of modern times. With James Callaghan at its helm, Britain faced a perfect storm of an incredibly cold weather including severe storms, industrial disputes led by trade unions which crippled the country demanding pay rises, out-of-control inflation, as well as a host of other issues which effectively made socialism unpallitable to the British public for the next 40 years, and it would take until Tony Blair's rebranding of *New Labour* before the Conservatives would again lose power from that point.

It seems now as though the political tables have turned; and the parallels to the current situation unfolding to the 70s are uncanny, though still markedly different in several ways. The 1970s were an altogether bleak decade for Britain: at its nadir, the British government even had to introduce a [three-day working week](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-Day_Week) in order to control energy usage, due to the coal miners' strikes, as well as the unfolding Gulf Crises and oil embargo by OAPEC countries. Whilst Britain finds itself in a very different position now to the 1970s, [many economists proclaim the eerie similarities](https://www.brookings.edu/blog/future-development/2022/07/01/todays-global-economy-is-eerily-similar-to-the-1970s-but-governments-can-still-escape-a-stagflation-episode/) between the current global recession and that period of time. By nominal GDP, the UK has remained steadfast in its position among world economies, still in fifth place, though trust in its economy no longer commands the respect it garned just half a century ago, where the British Pound still acted as the reserve currency around much of the World.

Almost half a century on, it seems as though the time to proclaim our second Winter of Discontent is now upon us, this time captained by Team Blue, long thought of as the more fiscally-responsible of the two political parties.

## Globalisation
It's no secret Britain has been in a state of gradual decline since the outbreaks of both its World Wars, which saw its crippling debts relinquish it of Empire, and with that, its commanding position on the world stage. Even the staunchest of nationalists would admit that Britain's golden days are a bygone era: the British Museum a Pantheon to former greatness, and souvenirs from our *"exploits"* around the World, presents a mere shadow of what Britain once was in the world. The world still sets longitude and its time according to the position of London, placing Britain at the centre of everything - perhaps the most lasting physical manifestation of a Eurocentric outlook on the world.

Recently however, **a rot has been exposed at the very heart of British society;** a rot which is rapidly infiltrating every nook and cranny of our fair Isles. Contrary to popular belief, this is more than an economic and political issue. Within society, the seeds of discontent have been sown leading to a series of irrational political decisions which recently have confounded many commentators, and yet these knee-jerk choices betray more hideous machinations than what one would suspect at *prima facie*. It reveals how democracies die.
