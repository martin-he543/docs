## Abstract

An increasingly relevant issue in many countries across the world, including the United Kingdom, is over
whether vaccinations should be compulsory. At the moment in the UK, despite vaccinations not being
compulsory, the rates of vaccination are pretty high. But it has become evident (such as with the recent increase
in measles cases) that more needs to be done to get people to take vaccinations. Many argue that vaccinations
should be made compulsory for the greater protection of society, and that without vaccinations, society would
succumb to pre-20th Century healthcare standards. Nevertheless, others argue that while this may seem to be a
reasonable argument, it compromises the rights of people, and as demonstrated by forced vaccination
programmes in other countries, don’t always bring about high rates of vaccination, but rather increase suspicion
in their governments. In this dissertation, we explored a few case studies concerning vaccination including the
fraudulent article in the Lancet and the Whooping Cough controversy, discover how other countries deal with
vaccination and the critical response of such, and interview several teachers with considerable experience
concerning the subject. Our results from our survey suggest that almost all respondents have had a vaccination,
but opinions were very much divided over whether vaccinations should be made compulsory, a good reflection
on public opinion at the moment. Ultimately, our research has concluded that vaccinations should not be made
compulsory, although we acknowledge that many people would think otherwise.

## Introduction

Vaccinations have been fundamental towards the end of widespread epidemics from infectious diseases
throughout the world, and it has been worldwide eradication efforts that have seen the end of illnesses that have
been plaguing humanity for millennia. Moreover, a study suggests that vaccines given this decade will help to
avert 23 million deaths by 2020. ([http://www.gavi.org/library/news/gavi-features/2013/vaccines-to-avert-more-than-23-million-deaths-by-2020/](http://www.gavi.org/library/news/gavi-features/2013/vaccines-to-avert-more-than-23-million-deaths-by-2020/)) As of now, in the UK vaccinations aren’t compulsory, but as vaccination rates
are heading on a downward trend, which has resulted in a recent insurgence of a host of diseases that had
previously been dormant for the past decade, that decision may be reversed. In the UK, childhood
immunisation plans which are given free of charge offer vaccinations covering around ten infectious diseases free
of charge, which have been proven to be highly safe, very great success. Therefore, given the proven safety and
efficiency of vaccinations, shouldn’t they be made compulsory, as they in almost all cases cause the person
receiving the vaccine more good than harm?

Although compulsory vaccination is the instinctive solution to under vaccination (as forcing people to get
vaccines results in significantly higher vaccination rates), it obviously unearths a plethora of ethical problems
(and as seen from other countries, problems concerning practicality). Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of
Human Rights (of which Britain follows by) founded following World War II states that “no-one shall be subjected
to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to attacks upon his honour and reputation.”
This is also found in Article 8 of the European Convention of Human Rights. Were Britain to implement such a
policy that forces vaccination, it would be a clear breaching of human rights in both agreements. However, a
problem which this brings up is a question of whose right takes precedence over whose. For example, in the case
of vaccinations, in the event of a parent and a child having two conflicting decisions, should the choice of the
parent supersede that of the child, and should the rights of citizen be stripped for the protection of the greater
society and populous, by producing immunity from a virus that may lead to its subsequent eradication?

## Case Studies

#### Case 1: falling rates of MMR vaccination due to fraudulent information

In 1998, a fraudulent research paper was published in the weekly peer-reviewed general medical journal The
Lancet. The paper, written by a research group led by Andrew Wakefield ((^2) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Wakefield) , rather radically suggested that the
three-in-one MMR vaccination (https://www.sciencebasedmedicine.org/antivaccine-hero-andrew-wakefield-scientific-fraud/) (targeting measles, mumps and rubella vaccine) was directly linked to inducing
symptoms similar to that of Autism, Wakefield collectively referred to these symptoms as “Autistic enterocolitis”.
We now know these allegations to be entirely based on unfounded claims and fabricated data – published for
reasons separate to science (finance, politics...). The aftermath of the endemic resulted in exponential drops in
MMR vaccinations in the UK, with the number of measles contractions increasing simultaneously. (https://www.theguardian.com/society/2013/feb/08/measles-outbreak-hits-18-year-high)

According to a parliament published paper: “Except for MMR, vaccine uptake in the UK is high and relatively stable
(see table). Between 1996 and 2003 MMR uptake fell by 10%. There is also considerable regional variation in MMR
uptake, ranging from 58% (Kensington and Chelsea) to 92% (West Cumbria). The current rise in measles cases is due to
local outbreaks where MMR coverage is low. Cases of mumps have also increased, though mainly amongst a cohort,
currently aged 13-22, who did not receive MMR or received only one dose.” Outbreaks of measles across England and
Wales are currently at the highest level they have been for the past 18 years, this is due to the fact that parents,
who were influenced by the MMR vaccination scare of 1998, refused to allow their children to be immunised
against measles and now that time has passed, the effects of this are beginning to show; with 2,016 confirmed
cases of measles in England and Wales in 2012: the highest annual total since 1994. ([http://www.parliament.uk/documents/post/postpn219.pdf](http://www.parliament.uk/documents/post/postpn219.pdf))

“People can think their teenager or young adult child is not at risk but that's not the case. If you were not vaccinated at two
and you're now 13, you're just as much at risk as when you were younger.” – Dr Mary Ramsay, The Guardian.

Had vaccinations been made mandatory, then such an under vaccination crisis fuelled by irrelevant unreliable
information and shock headlines from the media, would’ve been averted, which would have been better for the
general good of society, as less people would’ve die, and a herd immunity maintained.

#### Case 2: An individual case regarding 2 sisters in the UK (https://ukhumanrightsblog.com/2013/10/18/court-orders-mmr-vaccine-for-children/)

In 2013, the High Court came to a ruling that two sisters, against the wishes of both their mother and the wishes
of the two sisters, must receive the MMR vaccinations. It had come to the court’s attention following an
application submitted by the father ordering his daughters to both receive the MMR vaccination – it was fiercely
opposed by the mother. After the two parents of the girls (aged 11 and 15) had divorced, the girls lived with their
mother, while the father contacted them every alternate weekend and half the school holidays. Following the
publication of the discredited paper by Dr. Andrew Wakefield in the Lancet (See Case Study I for more information.) , both parents agreed to abstain
from giving their older daughter the booster MMR vaccination and entirely forego the other daughter’s one. But
since the Lancet had retracted the paper, the father became increasingly concerned about their decision to
forego their children’s vaccination, and in January, his solicitors wrote to the mother, asking for her consent to
their children’s vaccination, but following her disagreement, he submitted an application to the court.

Above all, the main consideration was the wellbeing of the children, so the court had to follow section 1 (3) of
the Children Act 1989. They rejected the mother’s appeal that it violated her human rights claiming it was
“repeated categorization of the course of immunization as non-essential invasive treatment.” The Court argued, rather,
that it was better characterised as “preventative healthcare.” In this instant, the judge citied expert Dr Ward:

>“a. Measles, mumps and rubella are serious infections, each of which carried an appreciable risk of dangerous complications
>in healthy individuals. Vaccination is the only practical way to prevent an individual from contracting infection, and all the
>evidence is that it is effective and has a very low level of side effects, which are generally mild and transient...

>d. With due consideration for established contraindications to vaccination in an individual case, it is otherwise in every child’s
>interest to be protected against measles, mumps and rubella with the MMR vaccine”

The father claimed that it was the Swansea Outbreak of measles that had happened that year that intensified his
concerns, and that prompted him to take action. In addition to this, in this particular story, one of the girls
happened to be a vegan, and one of the vaccine’s contents were derived from animal-based ingredients, in this
case, gelatine. However, the judge ruled that it was in their best interests to receive the MMR vaccination.

Some of the reasons behind the decisions, were that the daughters were overly concerned with the ingredients of
the vaccinations, rather than the consequences of getting one of the diseases, and the ingredients of the
medication that would’ve been administrated should they have fallen ill. Both girls had clearly also been
misinformed regarding the side-effects of the vaccinations versus the problems that getting one of the three
diseases posed on them. Also, the girls were clearly influenced by their mother’s views.

## Literature Review

#### Mandatory Vaccinations: Precedent and Current Laws - Jared P. Cole and Kathleen S. Swendiman (May 2014) (https://fas.org/sgp/crs/misc/RS21414.pdf)

Within this report, it outlines how mandatory vaccination laws are enacted in the United States, justifying them
as the preservation of public health is the primary responsibility of states and local governments. It specifically
mentions the case of Jacobson v. Massachusetts, in which the Supreme Court ultimately argued in favour for
mandatory vaccination. Massachusetts argued that their vaccination plans were for the greater protection of
society and that the vaccination program in the city of Cambridge “had a real and substantial relation to the
protection of the public health and safety.” The Court added that such laws were controlled by the State, and did not
> “contravene the Constitution of the United States or infringe any right granted or secured by that instrument.”

Jacobson argued in reply that his rights were being suppressed by the mandatory vaccination program, and that

>“a compulsory vaccination law is unreasonable, arbitrary and oppressive, and, therefore, hostile to the inherent right of every
>freeman to care for his own body and health in such way as to him seems best; and that the execution of such a law against
>one who objects to vaccination, no matter for what reason, is nothing short of an assault upon his person.”

This constitutional right is similar to those which you can find within the European Convention of Human Rights
and Universal Declaration of Human Rights. The court, however, rejected this claim as they argued

>“the liberty secured by the Constitution of the United States to every person... does not import an absolute right, to be, in all
>circumstances, full free from restraint.”

#### Should Childhood Vaccination Be Compulsory in the UK? – Miriam Fine-Goulden (Spring 2010) ([http://www.ucl.ac.uk/opticon1826/archive/issue8/articles/Article_Biomed_Sc_-_Fine-Goulden__Vaccination_Publish.pdf](http://www.ucl.ac.uk/opticon1826/archive/issue8/articles/Article_Biomed_Sc_-_Fine-Goulden__Vaccination_Publish.pdf))

This report argues that mandatory vaccination is should not be made compulsory in the UK. Due to downward
trends in vaccination, under vaccination is starting to become a major problem (exemplified from numerous
disease outbreaks), however, it argues that mandatory vaccination is not the best solution to this issue, for both
ethical and practical reasons. It supports this statement by using other countries’ mandatory vaccination
programmes as examples (such as the United States.) It brings up the issues surrounding whether or not
children or parents should have the final decision concerning vaccinations, and whether the rights of a society to
be protected from a disease supersede that of an individual (mentioning utilitarianism). Instead, it argues that
incentives such as financial rewards would be better (citing Australia as an example.) It concludes that parents
acting against a child’s interest requires a justified state intervention, and that choosing not to vaccinate your
child results not only harms your child, but puts others at risk. It also states that compulsory vaccination also
results in increased public distrust in the healthcare system and the government. In addition to this, it considers
that increased education and engagement with the general public is a better and more ethical way of increasing
vaccination rates and retaining the ‘herd immunity.’

## Methodology (Written using the American Psychological Association format of methodology.)
#### Participants
In this investigation, a questionnaire was sent to several secondary school teachers, male and female students
from 13-14 years old and miscellaneous participants close to the researchers of varied ages. Data was collected
from 80 participants in total, with 43 responses in the first week. Questions from the survey focused on testing
the understanding on the UK’s stance on vaccination in terms of Human Rights, and determining the popular
opinion on whether British citizens should be obliged to vaccinate or not – examples of questions asked are as
follows: “Do you think that vaccinations should be compulsory? What is your knowledge on vaccination
protocols? How much do you agree with the statement ‘everybody has the right to refuse vaccinations?’, etc.” It
is a recognised fault however, that the opinions of our respondents may not represent the general population of
Britain accurately, but by sending it to a range of different people, we tried to accomplish this.

#### Apparatus and Procedure

The researchers’ tasks were organised using a GANTT chart made on Microsoft Excel, which was also used to
create graphs of the data received from the Google Docs Questionnaire sent out via E-mail to the participants.
The interviews consisted of several teachers of subjects; Religion and Philosophy, Humanities and Science – this
was because the dilemma at hand was relevant to the field of expertise of these teachers and they could therefore
give educated and reliable responses to the questions asked.

Two different real-life cases were analysed, the details of these cases were found from online research – using
only reliable sources (e.g. scientific papers, government-published documents etc.) with all references
documented and cited at the end of this paper. The data and research primarily focused on matters in the United
Kingdom, with the British Human Rights Act of 1998 being applied to the situations studied. Any potential Bias
(e.g. confirmation bias, publication bias...) was avoided and both pro-mandatory and anti-mandatory
arguments were represented as impartially and logically as possible.

## Results

Then, we used the free web-platform Google forms to conduct our surveys. In order to eliminate as much bias as
possible, we muddled up the order of the questions, and the order of the answers to each question. We sent out
the survey to a large sample of 80 people with 43 responses – we sent it out to teachers, students, friends, and
other people we knew: male and female.

### What is your knowledge on vaccination protocols?

- Very knowledgeable — 2.8%
- Knowledgeable — 22.2%
- Decent understanding — 36.1%
- Adequate understanding — 22.2%
- Non-existent — 16.7%

### Have you ever had a vaccination?

- Yes — 88.9%
- No — 11.1%

### Jack is a teenager about to receive the meningitis vaccination. He wants the vaccine, but his parents object. What do you think should happen in this scenario?

- Jack's parents' decision should be allowed to override Jack's — 25%
- Jack should be able to choose for himself — 50%
- The vaccine should be given to Jack nonetheless for his own benefit (forced vaccination) — 25%

### Do you think vaccinations should be compulsory?

- Yes, they should be made compulsory — 44.4%
- No, this infringes on a person's human rights — 55.6%

### Do you think parents should have the right to override a child’s decision concerning vaccination?

- Yes – parents should make the final decision for the child — 13.9%
- Maybe – it depends on the situation — 58.3%
- No – the child should be able to pick what the child wants — 27.8%

### Do you agree with this statement – everyone has the right to refuse vaccinations?

- I agree — 69.4%
- I disagree — 30.6%

### What is your opinion on current vaccination procedures in the UK (i.e. not compulsory, but recommended)?

- I agree with them — 86.1%
- I disagree with them — 13.9%

### Do you think that fines and imprisonment (i.e. coercion) is a justified threat to those that fail to vaccinate themselves or their children?

- Yes – it is the only way to get people to vaccinate — 11.1%
- Maybe – it depends on the situation — 36.1%
- No – this violates a person's human rights — 52.8%

## Discussion

From the people that we questioned, the majority (88.6%) had received a vaccination over the course of their
lifetime. This is not surprising, as the people which we interviewed have been educated into getting vaccinations
as this benefits society as a whole, and the National Health Service recommends childhood vaccinations
(although it is not mandatory in the UK.) And the vast majority of people had some sort of understanding of the
vaccination procedures in the UK.

Almost everyone who everyone we asked agreed that the vaccination policies currently implemented (i.e. not
compulsory, but highly recommended and free) is good, which indicates that most of the people did not want
vaccination to be compulsory, but simply for it to be a recommended option. Although this is most certainly the
best ethical standpoint to take, there increasing levels of under vaccination which threaten our modern societies
free of illnesses that often spread rampantly during the previous century. In order to re-obtain the high levels of
vaccination that were usual before, mandatory vaccination is a consideration which the government could take if
the situation spirals too out of hand.

The majority of people also favoured the refusal of vaccinations. This is normal in the United Kingdom, but in
many other countries such as the United States, if you refuse vaccines, your child is denied basic public services
such as schools and day-care nurseries. The two questions concerning the conflicts of rights of parents and of a
child produced two different results. In the first question out of the two, more people favoured allowing the
parent’s decision than the second, possibly because the situation was put into context. The first question
favoured Jack to have the upper hand in choice, whereas in the second question, the majority stated that it
depended on the situation.

Finally, in the question concerning coercion, the majority of people were outspoken concerning the threats of
fines and imprisonment following the refusal of a vaccination. This is most certainly not a policy in the UK
(although it once was during smallpox outbreaks of the 19th century), but it remains a policy in other countries
of the world, such as Slovenia, and in Pakistan, you risk imprisonment. As a result, vaccination rates in Slovenia
are around 95%, one of the highest in the world, however, mandatory vaccination does not always produce such
good results as these, and as seen in Australia, a policy of economic awards may be a better course of action
achieving similar results without limiting the rights of an individual.

## Conclusion

So in conclusion we strongly believe that vaccinations should not be made compulsory in the UK. Based on the
results of our survey, we have strong evidence of the general British public agreeing with our conclusion. We feel
that when vaccinations are forced the patient's human rights are infringed and that their ability to make an
autonomous decision is being hindered, and so the forcing of vaccinations is also immoral and unjust.

We also feel that the threat of imprisonment and other crime-related punishments for avoiding vaccinations are
completely unreasonable, as the decision of the patient should be accepted as he is the one taking (or in this case
not taking) the vaccination. This moves on to parents and their role towards the vaccinations of their children.
We believe there should be no strict rule on whether a parent is allowed to overrule the child’s decision or not, as
either way there will be problems caused (the human rights of the child will be infringed or on the other end of
the spectrum the child may make unreasonable, dangerous decisions). Rather we feel that what is done depends
on the situation. Another idea we have is that age could be a potential decider for when children should be able
to override the decision of their parent – we believe that around 11 is a worthy age. Otherwise, a test could be
implemented to determine whether a child should make their own decisions. This test could include questions
concerning vaccinations in general, their use, separating the truth from the lies, and some general questions
involving the will of the child as well...

Finally, another reason we think that vaccinations should not be mandatory is due to its practicality. By making
vaccinations mandatory, not only are you sacrificing the rights of an individual and supressing their freedoms,
but you are increasing public distrust and hatred towards the government and public services. By encouraging
parents to vaccinate their children, and by increasing education to parents by letting them compare the dangers
of a severe allergic reaction to a vaccine (1 in 1 million) compared to the chance of death in say a measles
infection (1 in 500). Or, similar to the policies of Australia, you could implement an economic incentive to take
vaccines, that would certainly boost vaccination rates without the need of coercion.
