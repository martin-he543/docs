# Was the Home Front Changed More by the First or Second World War?

Both the World Wars helped to shape Britain into the modern society that it resembles today by drastically altering the Home Front (the conditions for civilians when their country is at war), politically, economically and socially. World War I saw the coming of the Industrial War as European Powers with vast territorial empires fought whereas World War II blurred the boundaries between the battle front and the Home Front – bring civilians to the front-line. I believe that World War II changed the Home Front more radically with long-lasting impacts.

The First World War radically changed the nature of warfare as the Government geared the country towards ‘total war’ – with the entire country focused towards the war effort. There were many social changes during the war. Though often overlooked, particularly foreshadowed by the Second World War, the Home Front suffered numerous attacks – notably at Scarborough, Hartlepool and Whitby which on the 16th December 1914 suffered 75 Zeppelin bombing raids that killed 119 people and caused mass panic – this attack was later employed by the Government in propaganda posters to portray the Germans as a barbaric people that would resolve to killing even innocent and defenceless women and children. This helped to exasperate the public into even stronger anti-German sentiments, (so much so that in 1917, the Royal Family changed their name from Saxe-Coburg/Gotha to Windsor amid growing suspicions from the public) that even culminated in occasional assaults on Germans residing in Britain. One odd remnant of this ‘war Germanophobia’ is even evident in dog breeds, for instance the German Shepherd was once called the ‘Alsatian’ in the UK as the name ‘German Shepherd’ was frowned upon at the time. These social changes affected many people during the war. From 1916, men were also conscripted into the armed force, and were forced to fight. The void left by men who went off to fight created a reduced workforce, so many women took the places of men in many jobs which before the war, many though women were incapable of doing such as farming, munitions factories of which 900000 women were employed in, and around 100000 women joined factions of the military such as the Women’s Land Army. Altogether, around 1.5 million extra women were working in paid jobs in a far greater range of occupations than before. These affected women as it changed their position in society, and how they were perceived, and altered significantly employment in Britain. Women also felt more liberated, as many wore short skirts, smoked in public, and had short hair – all things that would’ve been deemed unacceptable in Victorian cultural norms. The Great War put huge economic strains on Britain – during the war, Britain incurred debts equivalent to 136% of its GNP – an even after the war, the British economy did not recover to pre-war levels. Strikes in the years following the war were widespread, and unemployment reached a record high of 11.3% in 1921 as Britain entered its deepest recession in its history. Throughout the course of the war, the value of the pound had depreciated, with one pound in 1918 being worth half of what it had been in 1914. Furthermore, many countries cut off during the course of the war from a supply of British goods resorted to building up their own industries, and so come 1918, were no longer reliant on British goods. This contributed to contractions in many industries such as coal mining, and steel, and thus a growing number of Britain’s population during the inter-war period started to be employed in ‘white-collar jobs.’ Therefore, World War I had important long-term effects in moving Britain’s economy down the path from an industrial economy to an economy primarily reliant on the services sector. There were also big political changes in Britain. Following changing attitudes in Britain, in 1918, women over 30 were given the vote, and all men over 21 were given the vote, making Britain a more democratic society – ‘a home fit for heroes’ as many put it. This affected many people who previously did not have the vote as they could now influence British politics. As the country united together to fight a common enemy, many though that those more deprived in society should be helped, and the liberalisation of many ideas (as opposed to Victorian ideas that the poor stay poor due to laziness) concerning welfare provision led to changes such as ‘a right for minimum aid for disability or sickness,’ that benefitted many in need. Overall, World War I led to far-reaching change because it represented a shift from Victorian political norms and led Britain on a road towards modern society.

World War II saw the blurring to the battlefront and the home front as ordinary civilians experienced the horrors of modern warfare, experienced a year earlier in Guernica, Spain. There were many social changes. bombing raids plagued population centres such as cities as large-scale bombing frightened and killed many people – over 40000 were killed throughout the course of the Blitz. The damaged buildings and debris would leave many homeless and in the years following the war resulted in huge re-building projects. This led to the herculean task of evacuating many city children to the countryside. This socially impacted many as working-class children from the cities – many dressed in rags and suffering from diseases such as polio, got interact with many richer families who were shocked by their conditions. Other changes such as Social Security, the NHS (introduced through the National Health Service Act in 1948 which made doctors, hospitals, dentists and opticians available to everyone for free) and Council Housing have had long-lasting impacts in contributing to creating a welfare state. This affected many people from poorer backgrounds and led to many political changes – the years following the war saw the realisation of many proposals dreamed up years before. This liberalisation of ideas helped to transform the political landscape of Britain and brought Britain into modern political ideas. World War II helped to consolidate Britain’s demise as a world superpower, as the economic devastation following the war meant that Britain could no longer afford to keep its empire. The Marshall Plan displayed America’s dominance as a world power, and Britain’s economic demise. Overall, World War II led to big changes during and after the war.

In conclusion, I believe that World War II changed the Home Front more than World War I. Although many ground-breaking events happened; socially and politically, World War II had a larger impact on the Home Front. The movement of war to the Home Front helped to unify all classes of society which catalysed a shift in public mindset towards a welfare state the persists to this day.  Although politically, economically and socially, many changes came about from World War I, there wasn’t much change (e.g. the establishment of the NHS) until after World War II. Economically, however, I believe that World War I was more impactful as it put the economy on the breaking point culminating in a huge recession.
