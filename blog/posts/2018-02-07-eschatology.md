## Question 3
Liberal Christians would argue that hell is a spiritual place. They would suggest it is a relative period of eschatological suffering for the soul. It is also suggested by some that all will eventually make it to heaven.
Conservative Christians would argue that hell is a literal place. They would suggest it is a place of eternal suffering akin to an eternal fire. Some believe hell is a place underground.

## Question 4
Muslims believe life is a test of their iman (faith) and amal (practice of Islam). Nafs of the deceased are transported to Bazakh (an intermediate state) where their iman is judged. Believers of Islam will rest peacefully while the rest are in discomfort.
During Yawmuddin, the amal (deeds) of a person is assessed. Their good and bad actions are weighed as mentioned in Surah 21 “we shall set up just scales of on the day of judgement”. As such, a Muslim is naturally encouraged to live a virtuous life to preserve their fitrah and go to Al-Jannah.

## Question 5
On the one hand, an atheist such as Richard Dawkins would strongly agree with this statement. He would argue that not only is it unreasonable to believe in life after death, but it can be “a pernicious delusion” that has a negative impact on society. He would suggest that logical inconsistencies in the Bible as well as the Problem of Evil point towards disproving God’s existence, which by extension makes believing in life after death unreasonable. Furthermore, Karl Marx might suggest that religious ideas relating to the afterlife are the “opium of the people” and are utilised by the bourgeoise in order to exploit the working classes by making them more accepting of their plight in hope of being rewarded in the next life.
However, a theist would vehemently disagree with the statement. They would argue that religious scriptures don’t necessarily have to be logically coherent and that logical inconsistencies are the result of using excessive via positiva language to describe a God beyond human understanding. In addition to this, a mystic might argue that special revelations such as “Near Death Experiences” provide better insight into the afterlife rather than logical arguments and mystical experiences cannot be explained by reason alone.
Yet Kant would disagree with both aforementioned views. He would suggest that ultimately our lives are nihilistic, tragic and meaningless. In order to prevent this, we must utilise religious beliefs in order to remain virtuous and rid ourselves of our egoistic passions. Muslims would agree with the latter point, in how religion can be used as part of the internal war against one’s passions (enacting higher jihad) in order to preserve the purity of fitrah.
All in all, I would side in Kant’s view. This is because I believe there is not sufficient evidence to back first two views regarding the afterlife but I do believe Kant’s view provides an understandable approach to how religion can be utilised to lead meaningful virtuous lives.
