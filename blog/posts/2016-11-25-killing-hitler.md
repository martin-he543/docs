**Dear Jonas (Newmann),**

I would like to thank you for your solemn invitation to help in your plan to kill Hitler. Having inspected what you have earnestly asked of me, and having pondered the question you pose, I am ecstatic at your request for my assistance in carrying out your proposal. Your proposal concerning Hitler’s assassination is backed up with many valid points, especially concerning his wishes to conquer the entirety of Europe, and exterminate the Jewish population – as have been seen in his recent actions such as the invasion of Czechoslovakia, his Nuremberg Laws, and in many of the rants throughout his book: Mein Kampf. Likewise, I have always been outspoken in my criticism of the National Socialists and their Anti-Semitic policies, and it is with great certainty, that I say Hitler will attempt to fulfil his wishes over the course of the next decade, and the resulting war (which I believe Germany cannot win, especially if the United States intervenes) will result in a great loss of life with unforeseeable impacts – social and financial. Hence, I can fathom your reasoning behind your plan, especially considering the fact that you are a man of good intent with a good interest in ethics and morality like me. Despite this, and my evident loathing of Hitler, I am not able to help you.

One of the reasons why is because of my agreement with the ideas put forward by Kantian Ethics. Kantian Ethics adheres to very similar principles set down in Divine Command Ethics, such as ‘evil cannot be done to bring about good’ – i.e. the Pauline Principle. But rather than having to rely on an appeal to God (especially considering the enormous uncertainty in the existence of a God which Kant argues,) Kantian Ethics demonstrates the absolute deontological principles set down by Divine Command Ethics (DCE for short) through logical reasons and premises alone. Kant argues that these principles (such as don’t steal, don’t kill, don’t lie) can be explained through a thought experiment called universalisation which justifies whether an action is moral or immoral.  Universalisation is where you extend your action to everyone doing it and think about the logical consequences of that happening. If this results in an absurdity (reductio ad absurdum), then following the action is illogical, hence immoral. In this case, killing results in just this – by everyone killing each other, everyone dies, the human race goes extinct, and the concept of life is replaced by death ironically. Because of this, I cannot bring myself to kill Hitler, not only for egoistic purposes (i.e. I won’t kill Hitler for personal risks to safety and yours), but also for altruistic reasons, of which Kantian Ethics is – an absolute deontological ethical theory. Consequently, I view killing Hitler as an immoral, and illogical act for me to participate in.

Others would argue that killing Hitler is the obvious, moral act to perform; they would probably be relative consequentialists (such as Utilitarians and Situation Ethicists, but not egoists.) Utilitarianism (which derives from the word utility which means useful), may seem like the most intuitive solution to a problem such as our one regarding Hitler, but lacks much practicality and usefulness paradoxically.  One of the most commonly used phrases to sum up utilitarianism is “the greatest good for the greatest number,” although in Bentham and Mill’s cases (the two greatest advocates of utilitarianism), this only applies to humans. Utilitarianism, as mentioned before as a relative consequentialist hedonistic theory, doesn’t focus on the moral value of the action itself, but rather by judges it by its consequences and the amount of pleasure (and the reduction of pain) that a person receives. By these measures, a few can be sacrificed in order to save the many, and an action of wrongdoing (‘a necessary evil’) can be ignored if the result benefits a greater number of people. However, it is evident that utilitarianism is highly flawed and impractical. For one, it is impossible to measure what is the most loving thing as it is completely subjective (there is no such value of agape which you can have) - what is the greatest good in each scenario is a debate in its own right. Furthermore, the entire concept of utilitarianism relies on rating the morality of actions on their outcomes – it is impossible to predict the future [citation needed]. This means that abiding to utilitarianism relies on pure prediction - an enormous leap into the dark. * Because of this, it’s also impossible to ascertain whether killing Hitler (an inexperienced military leader, who will probably lose Germany the war) will save millions of lives and avert war, or whether an even more maniacal and experienced leader, such as Himmler, will take over, so it’s uncertain whether by killing Hitler you are committing a moral or immoral act. A utilitarian might defend their argument by claiming that ‘by not killing Hitler, you are killing 6 million innocent Jews.’ This is a slippery slope fallacy, because it suggests one event will inevitably culminate in a huge catastrophe without considering any intermediate events.

I believe that killing Hitler is an unnecessary risk to take when committing an act of homicide, having an angry German population set after you and potentially shifting history towards a bleaker, grimmer path. Thank you for understanding.

**Yours sincerely,**

**Martin Von Hermann**
