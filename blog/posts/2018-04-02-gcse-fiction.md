## Ozymandias, and London

In both Ozymandias and London, the misuse of power is depicted as detracting from the benefit those that reside in their respective societies. In Ozymandias, Shelley uses the hard consonant sounds of “sneer of cold command” reflects the oppressive nature of King Ramses II’s rule and how his undemocratic rule negatively affecting his people. This is further explored in “the heart that fed” - this suggests that he was driven by a greed for power, overlooking his subjects, instead obsessing over how his legacy was portrayed. Contextually, Shelley uses Ozymandias to criticise the English king of the time, King George III, who was similarly viewed as tyrannical and undemocratic. However, by setting the poem in a frame narrative, he attempts to distance himself from the criticism of George III by saying that someone else informed him of this. Similarly, London explores how the British government has failed to amend many of the woes for those that live in poverty-stricken London face on a daily basis. Blake blatantly utilises foreshadowing in “run in blood down palace walls” to allude to the French Revolution and imply that unless things are done to improve the plight of the working classes, the English will emulate the actions of the French.

In addition to this, both poems highlight how powerful individuals in the Church do not act in the interest of those in need. In Ozymandias, this is done through the phrases “king of kings” is used as a Biblical reference, referring specifically to Jesus. It suggests, like Ramses II, the Church isn’t acting for the benefit of the people. Contextually, Shelley was an atheist, and mocks the Church for being obliging, and even at times collaborative in the oppression of the working classes. Similarly, in London, Blake suggests that “every black’ning church appalls” - the lexical choice of the word “black’ning” implies that the Church is becoming increasingly corrupt or conversely could allude to how industrialisation driven by powerful individuals (i.e. capitalists) has resulted in a deterioration in the quality of life for many. In addition to this, the verb “appalls” arguing that the Church should be ashamed of what they have done. William Blake’s radical political views advocated that industrialisation caused a negative impact on people’s lives, leading to unnatural occurrences such as child prostitution.

Most effectively, both poems suggest that ultimately, all forms of human power are ephemeral, and that eventually, man will overpower nature. Shelley employs irony in “half sunk, a shatt’red visage lies” to suggest that even apparently-formidable symbols of power are “half sunk” and “shatt’red”, eventually not standing the test of time. As a Romantic poet, he believed in leading a life immersed in nature rather than the industrialisation of cities. Furthermore, “lone and level sands stretch” employs alliteration to emphasise the isolation of the statue, whilst the hypnotic effect of the sibilance of “sands stretch” depicts the vastness of the desert which overwhelms these worthless attempts at eternal power. London suggest with “chartered Thames does flow” that man attempts to take control over nature, with everything being the property of the rich. This is a criticism of capitalism, which brought about the rapid industrialisation of England in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## November 2017, Paper 1
Long, wooden planks, rotting out of disrepair, stretched out towards the horizon; uneven and creaking under its own weight, the structure groaned in agony at every passing gust of wind, bringing an early winter chill to the bones of the structure. A white bird sat perched on one of the tall, formidable beams of oak, chanting its melancholy melody, perforating the otherwise undisturbed stillness of the morning. An uneasy silence pervaded it all.

He cautiously proceeded down these planks, gazing in wonder at the heavy clouds that seemed to droop down, shadowing the peaks of the mountains, shrouded in mystery: mystery that was all too familiar to him. Yet as he proceeded further and further, taking it all in - the ripples that disturbed the pristine water and the pebbles that lay beneath - the clouds that had once offered solace to soothe his spirit began to transfigurate into the horrific image of a disfigured visage. He watched the water around him turn murky, murky with crimson red dye. The bird's melody turned into the sound of sirens, repeating over and over and over and...

It was late that evening, that fateful evening - June the 21st. After he listened to his father had expressed dismay for the umpeenth time, with his eyes fixed intently at the screen of their flickering television at every twist and turn in the stock market, Sean headed outside for his habitual Saturday night cycle. A particularly dense fog blanketed the heath that evening - Sean had to work particularly hard to steer clear of the oncoming traffic and when visibility was at best limited to the extension of his hand, this was rather challenging to do so. Nevertheless, he continued in his endeavour up the steep incline breathlessly.

The meek light of the sun shone through the dense fog. Just as he was about to reach the peak of the mountain, he heard the low eerie hum of a car in the distance approaching down that country road driving faster and faster. An ungodly screech erupted from the brakes of his bike as he made every attempt to control his bike, hurtling out of control. He swerved at the last minute, narrowly missing the car, falling into the spiky vegetation of the side of the road. He thumped onto the ground.

"Bloody hell!" pondered Sean out loud, observing that he had now punctured a tyre. The only option now was to walk back and return to back home by foot. Having cursed after the car one last time, he proceeded down the treacherous route back home with little conviction, giving up any hopes of being able to catch 9pm re-run of Friends.

But along the way, something caught his attention. He saw the lights of the lakeside cabin fully lit, blinding him as he walked past in a tyrannical fashion. Lights that hadn't been lit since the Johnsons moved into the city all those years ago.

Suddenly, a scream disturbed the stillness of the night.

A chill ran down Sean's spine, yet morbid curiosity had gotten the better of him and compelled Sean to run up to the window of the cabin. He crouched down and peered at two silhouettes struggle inside, one holding a dagger, the other holding a broken glass bottle. Behind him, he heard the door of the cabin burst open. Out came a clearly drunk old man with a weathered face.

"If ya' get any closer to me, I promise I'll call the c'ppers on yal," exclaimed the man, swiping the glass bottle in front of him, with slurred speech.
"Go ahead, give me your worst," replied the other man defiantly, stepping backwards towards the wooden boardwalk on the lake.

Sean gazed in sheer horror as the struggle continued onto the planks. The man with the weathered face dealt a blow to the other man's face. Blood trickled down from the gash on his cheek to stain his white shirt. Incensed by this, with the fury of a lion the other man charged utterly fearlessly towards the drunkard, and used his dagger to slit the side of his shoulder. Both men cried out in pain.

Their blood seeped out into the wooden planks and they continued even as the sun dipped beneath the horizon and the moonlight shone onto the vast lake. At that moment, one of the wooden planks gave way, encasing the drunkard's leg into a tight grasp and sealing his fate. Frozen in his expression, Sean continued in his trek homeward.

### Generation Weak - the Rise of Stifling Parenting
#### DORAN O’REILLY, Social Affairs Correspondent

It was a mere two decades ago that 95% of children between the ages of 7 and 11 walked to their primary school unaccompanied in the UK. 20 years later, that number has since plummeted to just 5%; this represents a wider trend at large of the increasingly ubiquitous, albeit stifling approach to parenting, that involves monitoring every aspect of a child’s life, quite ironic considering the vast amounts of freedom which parents who instill this approach experienced in their own upbringings. The result - a generation of children, more incapable, less able to solve problems than the last. This begs the question: have parents opted for a form of parenting that hinders the natural development of children and may actually harm their futures? Has overprotective parenting gone too far?

Let us consider the case of Isaac, a 12-year-old from Bolton, North England. When his parents discovered that he was meeting up with his classmates after school at the local park masked by the pretence of staying after school to improve his English, they unleashed such a wave of furor and anger, that they sentenced him to indefinite confinement in his room. It is likely that this partly explains what happened next. On the 29th January, his parents awoke to find that Isaac had committed suicide using a rudimentary noose constructed out of a skipping rope he had brought back from school. The pressures of modern life and school combined with his parents refusal to grant him the method to unwind contributed to Isaac’s untimely departure. Whilst this case is extreme in nature, it acts to show how when children are not given the personal space which they require and time to unwind, they find it increasingly hard to cope.

When will parents learn to not treat their children as if they were convicts in Guantanamo Bay? And will parents learn there’s more to a child’s development than grades? In an increasingly digital world, where children are constantly scrutinized and judged by their peers online on social networks such as Instagram and Snapchat which are making our children feel a lack of self-worth - the last thing which our children need is the further added pressure from their parents. Consequently, children are feeling as though the walls are closing in on them from all sides.

Yet, we can’t lay all the blame to parents. We, the media, are in part to blame for overprotective parenting. The recent growth in sensationalization of stories of crime and constant bombardment of fear mongering inevitably has made parents reluctant to allow their little gems, whom their have endearing cared for for a large portion of their lives, to go out and explore the outside world on their own. There’s no doubt parents want the best for their children, but perhaps the echo-chamber generated by social media and message chains has induced a paranoia onto themselves that has inflated the dangerous of the outside far beyond whatever they ever were. This was make crystal-clear to me when a friend of mine shared to me an absurd article documenting to me the dangers of “chicken-shop groomers” - it is obvious that these sensationalist headlines, the modern equivalent of Victorian penny-dreadfuls, is done purely for these online tabloid websites’ own self-interest: they clearly aren’t interested in the well-being of our children, but rather making a quick buck from online advertising. In actual fact, London’s streets have never been safer - crime is at an all-time low in 2018, and cases of child-kidnapping are all but a thing of the past.

All in all, as the next generation of children prepare to encounter unprecedented mental health challenges ushered in by the changing circumstances of life brought about by social media, we must all do our part to do what’s right for children. We as reporters must put our own self-interest behind making papers to sell. And, parents? I leave you with one request. Don’t be the parent who stifles a child’s development through overprotective parenting. Thank you.

## November 2017, Paper 2

(climbs onto podium assertively)

Education. I’m sure you’ve heard it all. The mandatory system of government legislation which we all must endure for fourteen years of our lives like a piece of food passing through the digestive system, only to sign up to at least three years of further education and about 30 grand extra in debt. It seems bothersome, and that’s because it is. From a young age, the vast majority of children have the following belief instilled upon then: if I do well in school, I can get good grades. “If I get good grades, I can get a good job. If I get a good job, I can get loads of money and buy and good wife. And happy wife, happy life?” Am I not right? Wrong. Yet it’s easy to see why so many people have such distorted perceptions about the education system, and perhaps life in general.

In fact, for the vast majority of human existence, humanity hasn’t functioned with an organised system at all. Living off a hunter-gatherer lifestyle, the lives of our predecessors focused more on the mere efforts to survive and reproduce, let alone attain grades. Modern society has bred a culture whereby success equates sucking up to a system which favours those that have resorted to the rote memorisation of vast quantities of ostensibly useless information. Yet the reality of success couldn’t been any more the opposite. Steve Jobs, one of the most visible examples of success of our generation dropped out of high school, instead opting to explore the exotic country of India, inspired by the teachings of Buddhism. Upon his return, not only did he come back with a clearer understanding of which direction to lead his life, but also filled a gaping void of disenchantment and nihilism that an overly-materialist society encourages. Clearly, travelling the world and putting our lives into perspective by bursting out of our self-constructed first world bubble can form part of an effective education.

The classrooms of today are more concerned with funnelling endless streams of easily-accessible information into the brains of children who’ve merely been taught to recall upon the content in the context of an exam, in the process lying to their future employers as well as themselves about their capabilities. With humanity on the cusp of new technological advancements by the day from the Internet that make information more accessible than ever, surely time would be better spent developing skills that cannot be learnt from a textbook? A Chinese saying by Confucius encapsulates this sentiment at best - “He who reads from the book is useless in practice”. The importance of having a creative mind has never been greater as machines threaten to deseat repetitive labour which can be programmed, as they did a century earlier to those concerned with mechanical labour.

Yes… so you’ve heard me rambling on about what made a bad education. But what forms a good education? A good education is one of balanced development; where children get to explore the outside world, not confined by the edges of a computer screen, but only inhibited by the limits of their creativity. A good education is one which inspires experimentation and equally failure, free from the fears of being deemed a failure or written off by people. But most importantly, a good education is one which promotes social interaction with others, allowing a child to naturally cope with others: evidently, we all have little control over what happens to us whether this be as a result of nature or other people, but it’s the manner which we react to these setbacks that make all the difference.

Perhaps, these ideas aren’t realistic in practice for the government to implement, as I’m sure they prefer having their conveyor belt of creative suppression to continue operating, but it’s always food for thought.

## Year 9, Horror Story

She yelled so awfully loud... like she was right in my face, you know?

My friend Ryan and I were about twelve and we had been to see Batman at the cinema. Jack Nicholson was awesome as the Joker and as we walked home we kept trying to laugh like that, swapping lines from the movie.

It was late and my stepfather was supposed to pick us up, but he never arrived, more than likely too drunk to drive. We waited around for him, but when the town clock tolled at midnight it was obvious he wasn't coming. Ryan offered to call his parents, but being so young, and out so late, it seemed more adventurous to walk home.

The main street was well lit, so we agreed to stick to it. Ryan's concern was walking past the park. Street lights were few and far between from there and most people stayed clear of the park at night, especially since the MacKenzie baby drowned in the pond, and they said the child could be heard crying sometimes. I told the kids at school I'd heard it myself.

We crossed the street to avoid the park. I stopped to tie my shoelace.

A slight breeze whipped through the tree tops, branches creaked, and one of the park lights began flickering.

Ryan said, “ Hey, Joey... Notice there's no traffic? “

I did notice and it was really quiet, too, but then again it was after midnight.

He said, “ If I hear a kid cryin' in that park, I swear I'll shit my pants. “

“ Can you hear that? “ I asked.

“ Hear what? “

“ Your imagination playin' tricks on you. “

“ But you said you heard that kid's ghost. “

“ Yeah, I did, but I didn't see anythin', and no-one believed me anyway, except you. “

We kept walking and made it to the corner. My house was half a block away, but Ryan still had two blocks to go, past the high school.

He looked back at the park and gasped. I saw it, too. More lights were flickering.

“ I don't want to walk home alone, “ he said. “ I'll give you five bucks to walk with me. “

“ Yeah, okay, “ I said, “ but I'm not holdin' your hand. “

At the high school, Ryan looked back at the park again. He looked frightened now. Every light in the park was off and it was pitch black.

“ It's nothin', “ I told him. “ Happens all the time. I see it from my bedroom window. The council needs to clean that place up. It's a dump. “

“ A dump where babies drown and their ghosts cry in the night. “

We kept walking and turned the corner. Ryan was relieved to be so close to home. He gave me the five bucks. Easy money.

Not far from his house was a little white chipped paint cottage, with a wire fence and rusty gate, and no curtains in the windows. All the lights were off and as we walked past an old lady started yelling, “ I see you there! Don't you knock and run! Don't you dare!  “

“ That's freaky, “ I said. “ She sounds really angry. Old people should be asleep at this hour. Lets go have a look. “

I started towards the gate, only joking, and Ryan grabbed my arm, saying, “ Don't, man. That's too much. She might call the cops. “

“ Don't you knock and run! “ she yelled.

“ She really hates kids, “ Ryan said. “ I've heard her before, but never seen her. “

His mother and father were still up when we arrived. They were disappointed that we had to walk. We should have called them. They offered to let me stay, but I declined, and just wanted to go home.

“ See you tomorrow, “ Ryan said, closing the door.

As I walked home, I had to pass the cottage again, and sure enough, she was still yelling, but then she stopped, as I stood at the gate.

I heard her say, “ I'm cold and can't get up. “

Now I was worried. What if she was injured? She might die and I could have helped.

I opened the gate and left it open, approaching the house. She sounded like she was crying now. At the front door, I knocked lightly, saying, “ Hello? Do you need me to get you an ambulance? “

“ I'm so very cold, “ she said.

“ I can get you help. “

I went to the window and peered inside. As my eyes adjusted, I became aware of something, and every bone in my body wanted to run.

“ I see you there! “ she yelled, but I couldn't see her. “ Don't you knock and run! “

The window boomed and cracked. I fell backward, gathered my feet, and didn't stop running until I got home.

My mother was in the kitchen as I came through the back door.

She said, “ You're as white as a sheet. How was the movie? “

I ignored her and went directly to my room and sat on my bed, grabbing my knees to stop them from shaking.

That house was empty.

## June 2018, Paper 1

Strong winds howl, engulfing the feeble, ill-protected town with great ferocity that could easily be capable of detaching a person – if not a building - from the ground. Slowly, a man, dressed head-to-toe in a worn-out straitjacket - offering him little protection from the unforgiving wind, proceeds down the Main Street taking great care not to be dislodged from the ground, or struck by the wintry waves – their never-ending onslaught thumping the shore like a drum in a carefree manner. He eagerly sought protection; the lacklustre town providing little choice. Finally, he finds shelter.

He opens the door of a shop with great difficulty, also letting in a sudden draught of cold air, enters soaking wet, moisture dripping off his face onto the polyester carpet periodically. Suddenly, a voice breaks the short-lived silence. “Hello. How can I help you?”  says the voice, in a loud beckoning voice, much alike the wind still howling outside.

“I’m fine, thanks,” the man replies.

“Would you be interested in a cup of coffee?” the voice replies, still obscured from view.

“That would be great, thank you!” the man replies, much to his delight, though still visibly disturbed by the absence of the source of the voice. Without warning, a tall man, formally dressed, appears behind him, startling him, holding a lukewarm cup of coffee.

“Coffee’s served!” the tall man exclaims rather monotonously. “You may call me Sam by the way,” he continues, handing over the cup of coffee abruptly, and walking to-and-fro in an uneven rhythm, loud footsteps emanating from his shoes.

“Well, I’m Shawn,” Shawn replies, drinking the cup of coffee, far too bitter for his liking, but nevertheless continues to down it hurriedly.

“I’ve been expecting you”, Sam replies. “Expecting you for a long time.”

Disconcerted, Shawn attempts to keep his composure. “How so?” he asks with a muffled sound. “How so?” he asks again, but with a more self-assured voice.

“You know,” he said, with a sinister tone. “Oh, you know.”

Discombobulated, Shawn took a step back towards the door, somewhat cowered. Even the uninviting outside made him feel more at ease than having this conversation with this tall man he’d just met, though who seemed to know a lot more about him than he did about the tall man. What could this tall man be talking about? Do we know each other, or is he simply misleading me, trapping me? Scaring me? Do I look like someone he knows? Shawn had only been in Littleworth for just over a week. How could it be that this man knew him, almost intuitively?

And then, it came back to Shawn.

## Shakespeare and Macbeth

Shakespeare presents fear in Macbeth in a variety of ways.

One way in which Shakespeare presents fear in Macbeth is through Macbeth’s own admission of his guilt towards the end of the extract. Macbeth no longer appears ambivalent about his fate, instead affirming that “they say, blood will have blood”. The use of chiasmus reinforces the idea of karma and the even-handedness of these actions – how one action warrants another. Contextually, this is reflective of Jacobean society who believed in the retributive nature of punishment in opposition to its use in rehabilitative purposes, in the absence of Enlightenment ideas. In an essence, “an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth”, a saying which can be attributed to Biblical teachings. Furthermore, binary oppositions were a commonly-held belief at the time – matters were to be seen in black and white – either morally deficient or pious in nature. In addition to this, the lexical choice of the word “will” on the one hand displays Macbeth’s certainty of his fate, but also foreshadows his downward descent into moral depravity. Finally, the trope of “blood” crops up throughout the play and is used in this instance as a symbol of guilt, and an emblem of his impending death. Thus, Shakespeare effectively conveys Macbeth’s fear.

Shakespeare also makes it apparent that Lady Macbeth is fearful of Macbeth’s outburst to have sparked the attention of the other lords. Her fears are exposed when she signals the lords to leave: “I pray you, speak not… But go at once”. It is apparent by the brevity of her sentences of her desperation to make as much of an attempt to cover up Macbeth’s delusions from the lords, by asking them to leave. The repetition of “at once” in the extract epitomises this mentality as she has been rendered into such a state of frenzy that she is unable to articulate herself well. In fact, she has been rendered into such a state of frenzy that she ditches her hospitable façade to reveal her true authoritative nature – the use of the imperative “speak not” employs a similar mannerism to the attitude she treats Macbeth during Act 1 where she promotes Duncan’s murder. Consequently, this makes for an exciting presentation of Lady Macbeth’s fear.

However, I believe that the Shakespeare’s use of Banquo’s ghost as a construct of Macbeth’s mind and embodiment of his guilt to be the most effective presentation of his fear. By portraying Macbeth in such a state of crazed paranoia, he is able to reveal his inner turmoil and sense of confusion. For instance, Macbeth feels the need to justify his masculinity towards the beginning of the extract – “What man dare, I dare…The baby of a girl” by the use of atypical similes such as “Russian bear” or “Hyrcan tiger”. These insecurities from Macbeth are only exacerbating by incessant questioning from Lady Macbeth and her tyrannical personality. However, towards of the extract, his false bravado attempting to justify himself as strong is stripped away to pleading in “Hence, horrible shadow! Unreal mockery, hence!”, to reveal his true fear of his actions coming back to haunt him, as happens subsequently. As such, this makes for a realistic depiction of Macbeth’s own inner fears.

## McDonald's Monopoly Speech

If you haven’t already noticed, it’s that time of year again when McDonald’s Monopoly rolls back into town - an assortment of irresistable prizes which you have a chance of winning by collecting stickers alongside your meal. But for every year which McDonalds Monopoly returns, new waves of criticism and backlash around the ethics of this marketing campaign arise. Is McDonalds Monopoly a fun, innocent game or does something more sinister lie behind each sticker?

On the face of it, it’s easy to see why McDonalds has drawn criticism around its Monopoly campaign. Annually, it attracts droves of people to its counters who otherwise might have opted for a healthier option instead of fast food and has been attributed to skyrocketing rises in child obesity. For an affordable price, you could have yourself a delicious meal along with the chance to win a prize – it appears to be absolutely irresistable. Obviously, McDonalds has attempted to respond to some of this backlash. Large meals no longer translate to a larger number of stickers, and technically, you have to over 16 to play McDonald’s Monopoly (which we all know is ridiculous and does little, if not nothing to deter the swarms of hungry 7 year olds clasping for hamburgers.) Yet these actions seem more to be token gestures as opposed to concerted efforts to curb childhood obesity which their business innately promotes.

Furthermore, McDonald’s Monopoly plays on natural misconceptions between the perceived chances of winning prizes and the immense value of the prize itself in a similar manner to the lottery. The actual likelihood of winning the top prizes is incredibly low. The way which the Monopoly game operates further acts to deceive players: whilst it is relatively easy to attain the first few stickers of a coloured group, there might only be 4 of the final sticker in circulation, and it is quite likely that those that do receive this sticker might simply throw it away! Finally, Instant Win stickers of food prizes dramatically skew your brains’ perception on the probability of winning. That Apple pie which you just won emboldens you to keep playing and dumping money into McDonald’s pockets, even if there are 12 million of such stickers in circulation.

Should the government step in to block McDonalds Monopoly on the grounds that it promotes childhood obesity? I strongly disagree. I’ve had many fond memories peeling off stickers from a meal, the ensuing drama and delight at having won an apple pie. Even if it’s incredibly unlikely to win the top prizes, it is undeniably good fun and McDonalds has consistently acted as a backdrop to treasured childhood moments that I will always cherish. But then, maybe again I’m just a victim of their elaborate marketing campaign.
